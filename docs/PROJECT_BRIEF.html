[SoG] Shoulder of Giants · project briefing · kept current · last updated 2026-11-25

Learn a simulator from a simulator, then correct it with reality

We are training one neural network to simulate household scenes: rigid objects, hinged parts such as drawers and doors, and cloth, with ropes and soft objects next. The scene is explicit 3D meshes, not video. The network first learns by copying a classical physics simulator across many objects and materials. Then it learns from real recorded motion, so that it ends up more accurate than the simulator it copied.

Why

Robots are short of data, and simulators are not real enough to fill the gap

Real robot data is slow and expensive to collect. Simulated data is cheap, but policies trained in simulation often fail on the real thing. The simulators are wrong in ways nobody can fix by hand. Friction and contact laws are approximations. Cloth and soft objects are the least accurate part of any engine. Above all, the physical parameters of real objects (mass, friction, stiffness, how a towel bends) are never known for the object in front of the robot.

If simulators were real enough, robotics would not have a data problem. A hand-written simulator can only be as real as the equations and numbers someone types into it. A learned simulator can take its realism from data and keep improving as real recordings arrive.

The bet

Chemistry already did this: pretrain on a cheap approximate solver, fine-tune on a little accurate data

Machine-learned interatomic potentials are trained on millions of cheap, approximate quantum calculations (DFT), then fine-tuned on a few hundred thousand accurate ones (CCSD(T)). The result is more accurate than DFT, the method it learned from (ANI-1ccx; MACE-MP-0 and UMA follow the same recipe). We bet the same recipe works for the physics a robot meets in a home.

The bet comes as three claims we can test and measure as scaling behaviour:

Claim 1

Coverage, not composition

Learned simulators already generalise to more objects than they were trained on. Generalising to new kinds of interaction and material should follow the variety of local interactions seen in training, not the number of trajectories.

Claim 2

Material read from motion

Leave the material unknown and the model should infer it from how things move, getting better the longer it watches. This ability should appear only when training covers many materials.

Claim 3

The student beats its teacher

A modest amount of real motion makes the model more accurate than its teacher simulator on held-out real interactions, and the real data needed falls as pretraining gets more varied.

What it is for

One model; leave different inputs empty and it becomes a different tool

Every input is a slot that is either given or empty: past frames, material parameters, forces. The model fills in whatever is empty. This is what makes one learned simulator useful for more than prediction.

Simulate

Give the scene, its materials and the gripper's path; get the future motion.

Identify

Leave the material empty and give past motion; get the friction, stiffness or weight that explains it.

Explain

Leave the force slot empty; get the push nobody measured, such as a hand the cameras missed.

Generate data

Produce training data for robot policies from a simulator that keeps getting more real as recordings arrive.

As research, the outcome would be measured scaling laws for learned physics (what kind of data buys generalisation), and evidence that a student model can exceed its simulator on real motion.

For now every input is given (decided 2026-11-24): rigid networks fit better that way, and cloth keeps its shape far better running alone. The first job is a simulator that predicts well; identifying and explaining come back once it does.

How it works

The network predicts forces; exact physics moves the vertices

Each object is a mesh. Every frame, a transformer looks at every vertex: its recent positions, its neighbours, the material slots and the gripper's next move. It outputs a force on each vertex. A small exact layer then does what we can write down with certainty and what the network has shown it cannot learn precisely: gravity, F = ma, air drag, vertices held by the gripper, and contact with fixed surfaces such as the table. The new positions join the history and the loop repeats.

Why forces and not positions directly? With an exact layer underneath, a model that outputs nothing still obeys gravity and still rests on the table, and the physics carries over to real objects: on 93 real ramp slides (Physics 101), the physics layer alone matches the real motion to 1 mm, while purely learned models never slide at all on the real ramp (82 mm off). Training then taught our own model to brake (26 mm), one of the open problems. Both networks as built today, and the measurement behind each choice, are on the architecture page.

As of 2026-11-24 this design holds for cloth. For rigid objects the best network so far predicts motion directly, with no physics layer, and where the layer comes back is an open question (see Where it stands).

What came before

Four ways people get physics for robots, and where each runs out

Hand-written simulators, tuned to realityMuJoCo, Isaac / PhysX, Newton; system identification; per-object digital twins such as PhysTwin
Exact when the equations and parameters are right. Our teacher reproduces real ramp slides to 0.7 mm.
Parameters are unknown for a new object, and the equations themselves are approximate. Two of Newton's three cloth solvers simulate a different cloth from the one they are given.
Learned simulatorsGNS (2020), MeshGraphNets (2021), FIGNet, SDF-Sim, WorldParticle, RiCo and PhysiFormer (2026)
Copy a simulator well and compose: RiCo runs 270 objects after training on 10 or fewer.
Almost all stop at their teacher. Most cover one kind of object, take no actions, are told the material rather than inferring it, and are never fine-tuned on real motion. The broadest, WorldParticle (SIGGRAPH Asia 2026), covers cloth, elastic solids, fluids and sand with one design but a separate network for each, no robot actions and no rigid objects, and shows real cloth only by eye.
Particle models learned from real videoPGND, AdaptiGraph, DPI-Net
Learn from real data directly, including cloth and rope.
Small data, usually one object or scene at a time; no shared physics prior to start from.
Video world modelsCosmos, Wan, V-JEPA
Trained on internet-scale video; look plausible.
No explicit 3D state and no physical parameters to set or read, so nothing to correct when the motion is wrong.
What is new here. The backbone (attention over mesh vertices) is shared with recent learned simulators and is not the contribution. The differences are:
  1. Inputs that are each given or empty, so one model simulates, identifies materials and infers unseen forces.
  2. One action-conditioned model across rigid, hinged and cloth objects, with the gripper in the scene.
  3. Distil first, then fine-tune on real motion, and measure whether the student passes its teacher.
  4. Claims 1 and 2 measured as scaling behaviour, not as architecture features.
How we got here

Rigid objects, then cloth, then small tests that found the causes

  1. 2026-10-16 to 10-20

    Rigid objects first

    The rigid teacher was chosen by a bake-off of Newton's solvers and checked against closed forms before any data was made. The first models beat constant velocity where things move, and the physics layer is what lets them slide on a real ramp at all. A graph-network baseline copies the teacher more closely, though, and rigid-only misses the point of a learned simulator, so the project moved to cloth.

  2. 2026-10-20 to 10-29

    Cloth, and the first real comparison

    Two of Newton's cloth solvers failed the teacher gate; the explicit integrator passed, and generated 20k cloth episodes. On real tracked cloth (PGND), the 64M network fine-tuned on real recordings comes within 2 mm of a teacher whose material was fitted to each recording.

  3. 2026-10-30

    Ropes and soft objects gated

    The same explicit integrator passes rope and soft-body teacher gates, ready for the next data.

  4. 2026-11-06 to 11-21

    Fit first

    Before scaling anything, find out why the network does not fit its own teacher data well. Every suspect but training length was ruled out, and training and held-out errors stayed equal, so more episodes of the same kind were not the answer.

  5. 2026-11-22 to now

    Small tests find the causes

    A one-episode test showed the cloth network could not fit even one episode: in fixed world directions every orientation is a new example. Describing each vertex's surroundings in its own directions fixed that and took the full-data fit from 0.69 to 0.59 of the simple guess's error. The same approach on rigid objects showed that the physics layer's own answer was the obstacle wherever the gripper pushes. A plain transformer without the layer, trained on 128 episodes, then matched baselines trained on 50,000. On 2026-11-24 we decided to train with every input given first.

What makes it possible

Five things that were not all true two years ago

GPU simulators make teacher data cheap

Newton runs thousands of worlds in parallel: our 20k cloth episodes took 6.8 hours on one GPU. Variety is now a choice, not a budget.

Real 3D motion with ground truth exists

Hands and objects with motion capture (HOT3D, ARCTIC, OakInk2) and tracked real cloth (PhysTwin, PGND) give the real data that fine-tuning needs.

Learned simulators compose

Local, relative encodings let a model trained on a few objects run hundreds (SDF-Sim, RiCo). Composition is largely solved, which leaves coverage as the open question.

Sequence models that condition on anything

Diffusion forcing and related work let one model take any subset of frames and quantities as given and generate the rest, which is what the given-or-empty slots need.

A recipe that already worked elsewhere

Atomistic machine learning showed that pretraining on a cheap solver and fine-tuning on a little accurate data beats the solver. Robotics has the same structure, with Newton playing DFT.

Where it stands · November 2026

Rigid scenes now fit from 128 episodes without the physics layer; cloth still fits its teacher loosely

Rigid objects, copying the teacherOpen

A plain transformer with no physics layer, trained on 1,024 episodes, is ahead of baselines trained on 50,000, one frame ahead and over seconds. In busy scenes it still misses rare events such as a topple.

Rigid objects, things at restOpen

Without the physics layer nothing slides on the real ramp (82 mm off; the layer alone is within 1 mm), and shut drawers drift open by 27 mm.

Cloth, one frame aheadOpen

The best network removes about half of the simple guess's error (0.475). More data does not help; longer training, the local frame and giving it every input do.

Cloth over secondsWorks

14, 50 and 77 mm from the teacher after 1, 2 and 3.8 seconds on unseen episodes; 5 of 336 cloths blow up, and the rest keep their shape (stretched 1% at the median). Not yet a usable simulator.

Real clothWorks

After fine-tuning on real recordings, 17.6 mm from the observed cloth at 2 seconds, against 15.8 mm for the teacher with fitted material. The third claim (beating the teacher) is not shown. The same fine-tuning went unstable on the longer-trained network (26 mm).

Reading material from motionDeferred

Has not appeared. From 2026-11-24 networks train with every input given, and hidden inputs come back once the simulator itself works.

Ropes and soft objectsReady

The teacher passes both gates against closed forms; Newton's other solvers fail them. No training data generated yet.

Rigid objects. The network that works is the simplest one we tried: a small transformer with no physics layer and every input given. Trained on 1,024 episodes it predicts unseen scenes better than baselines trained on 50,000, both one frame ahead and running alone for seconds. Longer training improves single frames (nine times the training: 0.37), though no longer how it runs alone; more episodes improve how it runs alone. Running alone it ranges from near-exact to wrong. In its best unseen scene it stays within 3 mm of the teacher for six seconds (first clip). In the median one it ends 39 mm off. In its busiest it follows for two seconds, then leaves standing a book that the gripper knocks over (second clip). Our original design, a network that predicts forces for an exact physics layer, loses wherever the gripper or a second object is involved. The layer still holds things at rest better: a shut drawer, an object on the real ramp.

Cloth. The best network describes each vertex's surroundings in the vertex's own directions. It keeps the cloth's size and shape over seconds, on teacher data and on real cloth, and after fine-tuning on real recordings it comes within 2 mm of a teacher fitted to each recording. One frame ahead it still leaves half of the simple guess's error. More data does not change that; longer training does: training for as long again took it from 0.59 to 0.52, brought its rollouts 12 mm closer at 3.8 seconds, and also brought it 6 mm closer to real cloth before any real data, a lead the same real fine-tuning then lost (it went unstable). A third stretch took it to 0.49, closer again over seconds, but with more cloths blowing up (21 of 336 against 6). The same third stretch trained with every input given does not grow that tail: 5 blow-ups against 28, at 0.475 on one frame, so it is the cloth network to build on. The network follows an unseen fold (first clip) but not yet a shake (second clip); the third clip shows what giving every input fixed.

What's next

Fit, then decide on data, then which data, then real-data training

  1. Now: Fit the teacher data, with every input given. One frame ahead, cloth is at 0.475 of the simple guess's error and rigid objects at 0.37. For both, longer training still improves single frames but no longer how the network runs alone: more cloths blow up, and rigid replays end farther off. Dropping the physics layer, which made rigid objects fit, does not help cloth: it ends level on one frame and rolls out much worse, so cloth keeps it. Giving cloth every input fits one frame as well, and running alone its cloth stretches a fifth as much (12% against 61%); cloth now trains that way. Practising running alone for longer during training does not stop the blow-ups (25 to 44 of 336 unseen episodes as the practice grows), and rigid objects trade single-frame fit for a few millimetres. Training cloth's third stretch with every input given does stop them (5 against 28), and a loss on the network's own 3.3-second runs halves them where they appear. Running: a fourth stretch of cloth training with every input given, and that loss on the best network.

  2. Rigid objects: scale up the plain transformer. Longer training improves single frames and more episodes improve the rollouts; a network five times larger is worse at both training lengths tried. Three times longer again took one frame from 0.45 to 0.37, but its replays end a few millimetres farther off. Open: holding things at rest without the physics layer. On the real ramp it probably needs training windows that start from rest, as the real test does: the ramp episodes do show objects let go on a slope, but training starts a frame later. Running: a test of that.

  3. Scale data beyond 20,000 episodes? Not for now. For cloth, ten times the data is worth 0.015 at full training, and the full-data network does as well on unseen episodes as on its own. The question returns when a network does better on its own episodes than on unseen ones. The rigid network did so on 128 episodes, and eight times the episodes closed that gap and made its rollouts closer.

  4. Which data: the coverage test. Claim 1 says variety of interactions matters more than trajectory count. A first test on 2026-11-22 could not tell them apart, because none of its networks fitted even what it trained on. We repeat it when one does.

  5. Post-train on real motion. Real cloth is within 2 mm of the teacher fitted to each recording but not past it, which claim 3 needs, and the fine-tune must first be made stable for the better network. Then hands and objects from HOT3D, ARCTIC and OakInk2.

What has already been ruled out, and the numbers for each item above, are on the test results page.

How we work

Rules we learned by getting them wrong first

  • Gate the teacher before generating data.A dataset inherits every defect of its teacher. The first 16k cloth episodes bounced, crossed themselves and exploded in piles, none of which the original gate tested.
  • Judge a solver by closed forms, not by whether it settles.Two of Newton's cloth solvers were stable, settled nicely, and simulated a different material from the one they were given.
  • Compare paired, with an interval.Score two models on the same windows and bootstrap the difference. Adjacent checkpoints can differ by 10 mm over 2 s, and two sets of 300 test windows by 0.1.
  • Learn, don't build.The exact layer holds only what the network demonstrably cannot learn. Fix a model failure with data, losses or architecture, not with more solver parts.
  • Check a score used as a loss on its blind side.Trained on a one-sided distance to real cloth, a model "won" by inflating the cloth 17,500 times its area. Report both directions, and strain, beside any score.
  • Run, don't assume.Print the trajectory before theorising. Five plausible explanations of one bug failed in a row; looking at the numbers found it.
  • Match the comparison to the claim.The latest lesson: a short-training network made the fit look worse than it is. State how long a model trained beside any claim about what it can learn.
