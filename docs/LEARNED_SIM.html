Give it what you know. It simulates the rest.

A learned simulator for household scenes. It reads and writes 3D meshes, never pixels. Every input is a slot that is either given or empty, and the model generates the empty ones, such as the next quarter second of motion, a friction nobody measured, or a push nobody saw.

Cloth

Rigid objects alone would miss the point of a learned simulator, so phase 3 moved to cloth. Of Newton's cloth solvers only the explicit one simulates the towel it is given (the cloth page). Its episodes are towels and napkins lifted, dropped, dragged, folded, shaken, pushed and slid by a gripper, and each material property changes what the model sees (the motion and the gripper's force) in 68–92% of actions.

The learned towel holds still and keeps up for a second, and it has started to read a material from motion, which no rigid model did. Over two seconds, lifted and laid towels turn to rubber. B2 stretches as much, so the cause is in training, not in the physics layer. The likely culprit is the training noise: it jittered each cloth vertex by itself, stretching the inputs by 7%, where a rigid body's noise moves the body whole.

Rest had to be built in

A force model starts in free fall. To leave a cup on the table it has to cancel gravity exactly, and every recipe we trained first also turned resting objects by about 1.4° per step, which a position-only metric hid. A default that holds a touching object up by its own weight, centred under its centre of mass, removed the rotation. A loss that weighs small errors linearly and large ones quadratically, plus training on the model's own six-frame rollouts, kept most of it through training.

Next

  • Materials from motion. The cloth model reads stretch; density, bending and friction not yet, and the rigid models read nothing. This is claim 2.
  • Hinged parts. Lids and drawers that follow the gripper.
  • Real data. Cloth handled by hands and robots (PhysTwin, PGND); for rigid objects, hand-object recordings, where only 2% of OakInk2 is hand-free motion.

No camera anywhere. The simulator reads and writes 3D state. Video matters only upstream, when real recordings are turned into mesh motion for training, and that tracking step sits outside the model.

Parameters are physical quantities in fixed units (kg, friction coefficient, restitution, Pa), so they mean the same thing in Isaac, in Newton and in a real kitchen. Solver settings do not transfer: this repo measured one damping value giving different bounces at different masses and contact stiffnesses.

The network predicts forces. Physics moves the vertices.

The full design as it runs in the code, with shapes, formulas and the measurement behind each choice: Household Simulator Architecture.

1
Tokens learned
One per vertex per frame in the window: rest position, normal, position, velocity, force slot, noise level. One per object: role, type, and each parameter slot with its own noise level.
2
Processor ×6 learned
  • Time. Each vertex attends to its own earlier frames in the window.
  • Space. Each vertex attends to its mesh neighbours and contact neighbours at that frame. Edges carry the offset now, the offset at rest, both normals and the relative velocity.
  • Object. Vertices write to their object token and read it back. This is where a material gets worked out from motion.
Contact neighbours are re-found from the current positions at every frame.
3
Heads learned
  • A force on every contact edge, applied equal and opposite to its two ends.
  • An internal force on every cloth vertex, summing to zero over a cloth that nobody holds.
  • A force on any vertex whose force slot is empty.
  • A value for every empty parameter slot, read from the object token.
4
Physics layer exact
  • Add gravity and the given forces.
  • Rigid: sum into one force and one torque, divide by mass and inertia.
  • Hinged: the same, then keep only motion along the joint.
  • Cloth and soft: each vertex by its own share of the mass.
  • Contact with fixed surfaces by default: the surface pushes back and grips with friction up to μ times the push; the network adds everything else.
  • Step one frame; the next frame starts from the result.
5
Output generated
Positions for the next frame and values for the empty slots. Repeat frame by frame for as long as the rollout should run.

Why predict forces

  • A given mass or a given push has its exact effect, even outside the training range.
  • Contact forces are equal and opposite, so momentum is conserved by construction.
  • Rigid objects stay rigid by construction. The network learns only what physics leaves empirical: contact, friction, deformation.

This is a bet, tested against the same network outputting positions directly. GNS and MeshGraphNets predict accelerations and integrate them; Neural Gaussian Force Fields (2026) predicts force fields.

Cost follows moving vertices, not scene size: attention is local, static surfaces join only near motion, and objects at rest sleep until something touches them. Starting sizes: about 200 vertices per mug, 1,000 per towel, 16 neighbours per vertex.

Past motion is optional, and length is unlimited.

  • Give 0 to 8 past frames. Training cuts windows anywhere in long episodes, so starting from rest and joining mid-motion are both ordinary cases.
  • Predict one frame, append it, slide the window one frame, repeat. The network only ever sees one window, so any length works. (The first design denoised 8 frames at once; stepping one frame at a time was 5 mm off after 8 frames where denoising was 26 mm, overfitting one episode.)
  • Only parameters outlive the window. Physics continues from positions, velocities and parameters. Velocities come from the frames in the window; parameters live in the object token and are carried forward. Training sometimes puts a little noise on past frames so the model never trusts its own output exactly, the same job GNS's noise injection did.

Train by hiding things and filling them back in.

  1. Teacher episodes in Newton, with the Kamino solver: random tabletop scenes, objects and materials, a scripted force-capped gripper, occasional pokes at random vertices, planks to slide on, drawers and lids. (Isaac Sim waits for a driver update.)
  2. Cut windows anywhere in those episodes and give 0 to 8 past frames.
  3. Hide slots at random. Each parameter half the time. Some of the pokes too, so the model learns to find unseen pushes and learns that they are rare and local.
  4. Perturb, then take one physics step. Each later frame of the window gets its own level of rigid noise; the model steps each frame once and is scored on the true next frame, so it learns to pull a wrong state back. Some frames are the model's own rollout of up to six frames, scored the same way. Hidden parameters and hidden forces are scored against their true values.
  5. Fine-tune on real recordings turned into mesh motion: motion-capture poses for rigid and hinged objects, tracked cloth. Parameters and unseen forces stay empty, so real data corrects only the shared physics.

What the first experiments decide.

  • Forces or positions. The physics layer against a network that outputs motion directly. So far: on the teacher's own scenes the direct networks fit rigid objects better, including masses three times heavier and pokes twice as hard; on real slides only the physics layer works (27 against 81–82 mm); for cloth it holds a resting towel (0.05 against 4.2 mm over a second).
  • Vertex or face contact. FIGNet needed faces for flat-on-flat rigid contact on coarse meshes.
  • Contact at 30 fps. A dropped mug lands within one frame, so contact acts as an impulse. Settled so far: velocities averaged over the frame match the teacher's impulses, and no substeps were needed.
  • Memory. One friction sample per rollout, as in the toy, or a distribution refined as observed frames arrive. The cloth model predicts a distribution per empty slot; rollouts sampled from it spread 1.2–1.7 times their error over one frame.
  • Unknown forces can explain any motion. If real fine-tuning leans on them, allow them only near hands.

Rigid models trained 17–20 Oct 2026 on 62,826 Kamino episodes, cloth models 21 Oct 2026 on 20,032 episodes of Newton's explicit cloth solver; the time figure is still a hand-written illustration, not model output.
  • MeshGraphNets: mesh edges plus world-space contact edges
  • GNS: local message passing, noise injection for long rollouts
  • FIGNet: face-to-face contact for rigid bodies
  • Diffusion Forcing: per-frame noise levels, rollouts past the training horizon
  • Simformer: one diffusion transformer, any slot observed or latent
  • Unified World Models: separate noise levels give policy, forward and inverse models
  • Neural Gaussian Force Fields: dynamics as learned force fields plus integration
  • PhysiFormer: whole-trajectory generation over mesh vertices
