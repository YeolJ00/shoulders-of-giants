Two networks, one recipe; only cloth keeps the physics layer

Cloth and rigid objects each have their own network today, built from the same transformer block and trained the same way: look at the last eight frames of every point in the scene, predict the next frame, and repeat. The cloth network predicts forces and lets exact physics move the cloth. The rigid network predicts motion directly, because the rigid physics layer made it worse. This page says what each one reads, computes and outputs, and which measurement decided each choice.

One step

Read the last eight frames, predict the next, append it, repeat

Every point in the scene is a token in every frame: cloth vertices, points on each object's surface, the gripper, and the table and fixed objects near anything that moves. The network sees no absolute positions. It sees motion (velocities, offsets to neighbours) and where things are only through the attention's rotary positions. After the trunk the two networks part.

The block

Attention across the scene within a frame, then along each point's own past

Both networks stack the same block, borrowed from the Qwen3 language models: normalise, attend, add back; grouped-query attention with normalised queries and keys; a SwiGLU feed-forward layer; no biases. Ours splits attention in two. Space attention lets every point in one frame look at every other point in that frame. Time attention lets each point look at its own earlier frames only. A cloth window has about 1,560 points in each of 16 frames, so each token attends to about 1,560 others plus at most 15, instead of all 25,000.

Cloth network

Each vertex reads its surroundings in its own axes, and outputs a force

A cloth vertex's input describes everything around it as seen from that vertex: its velocity and acceleration, the offsets and stretch of its eight grid neighbours, where the gripper will move next, gravity, and the nearest contact and fold. All of these are written in the vertex's own axes, built every frame from its neighbours. That makes a towel turned on the table the same example as before, which the network otherwise had to learn one orientation at a time. The vertical part of every vector is added beside it in world axes, because gravity makes "up" special.

What it outputs

  • An internal force and a contact force for every cloth vertex, in units of the vertex's weight. A third output adds force along world up; all three start at zero. With nothing holding the cloth, the internal forces are made to sum to zero.
  • The material (weight, stretch, bending, friction) as a mean and an uncertainty, from the whole cloth. It matters only when the material is hidden; every input is given for now, so this output gets no training at the moment.
  • How large its own one-step error is likely to be.

What the exact layer does

  • Divides force by each vertex's mass, adds gravity and air drag, and steps the position forward.
  • Keeps the cloth out of the table, floor and plank, and applies friction against them and against scanned objects, capped by the whole normal push, whoever supplies it.
  • Moves held vertices with the gripper.
  • Everything else is learned: how the cloth resists stretch and bending, how it touches itself, how the gripper's fingers push it, and how scanned objects hold it up.
Rigid network

A plain transformer over surface points that predicts motion directly

Each object is a cloud of 48–600 points on its surface; the gripper, drawers and lids are points too. A point reads 54 numbers: its velocity, its place on its own object, the force the gripper applies there, and what kind of thing it is. Points on moving objects also get 34 numbers about what is nearest within 10 cm: the closest table point and its height and normal, and the closest point on another moving object or the gripper with its relative velocity. Each object's material (mass, friction, bounciness, centre of mass, inertia, damping) is added to all of its points.

The network outputs an acceleration for every point. Each point is stepped forward with it, then each object is fitted as one rigid body to its points' new positions (in training each point is scored directly). There is no physics layer: the version with one started worse than predicting constant velocity (see below).

Drawers and lids are not held to their rail or hinge. In this network they are fitted as free rigid bodies like any other object, which is consistent with shut drawers drifting open by 27 mm in its replays. The physics layer kept them on their joints; the network without it does not yet.
Training

One physics step from a slightly wrong state, scored against the teacher

Each training window holds 8 true past frames and 8 frames to predict. The frames to predict are first pushed off the truth by a random walk, 1 mm per frame for cloth and 3 mm and 0.015 rad per frame for each rigid object, each frame scaled by its own random level. The network then predicts every next frame from the perturbed one before it and is scored against the teacher. It learns to pull a slightly wrong state back, which is what running alone needs. Without this noise the rigid network learns nothing (0.98 of the simple guess's error).

Pushforward: in every window the first 1–4 frames (cloth) or 1–6 frames (rigid), a random number each step, are replaced by the network's own predictions, so it also practises correcting its own errors, not only random ones. A trial running now asks whether letting it practise for up to half a second (cloth) or a second (rigid) stops the long-horizon blow-ups and drift.

Losses

  • Both: distance plus squared distance from the teacher's next frame, in centimetres, so small errors pull linearly and large ones dominate.
  • Cloth also: the error of each mesh edge's length against the teacher's, relative to its rest length, and the uncertainty output's error.

For now every input is given

The material, the gripper's force and all eight past frames are always given. Hiding them is what the material-from-motion claim needs, but it cost the rigid network a third of its fit (0.77 against 0.57), so it waits until a forward simulator fits. Cloth windows are turned and shifted at random; for the rigid network that is optional and acts as extra data.

Running alone

At test time the network starts from 8 true frames and then feeds itself: each step reads the latest 8 frames, its own predictions included, while the gripper and the table follow the recording.

Open problems

Where the architecture is known to fall short

  • Running alone. Longer training still improves one frame but no longer the rollouts: more cloths blow up after landing, and rigid objects keep sliding after the gripper lets go. The pushforward trial tests a training fix; the next candidate is teacher labels for the states the network reaches on its own.
  • Rest without a physics layer. The rigid network never slides on the real ramp (82 mm off, where the friction law alone is 1 mm). Its data never shows an object let go on a slope.
  • Joints. Drawers and lids are fitted as free bodies (above).
  • Where the gripper drives contact. The cloth layer costs most on the push, where the gripper drives the contact (without the layer 0.77 against 0.92 on that family). The rigid network does not follow objects the gripper knocks over.
  • Material from motion. The material output is idle while every input is given; claim 2 needs it back.
