[SoG] Shoulder of Giants  ›  Making the training data

[SoG] · how the data is made · as of 2026-11-25

Every training example is a run of a classical simulator, recorded

The network learns by copying a teacher: NVIDIA's Newton physics engine, run on GPUs. A generator builds a random tabletop scene, gives every object a random material, plans what a gripper does, lets the teacher simulate it and records the result at 30 frames a second. There are two datasets, one for rigid and hinged objects and one for cloth. Real recordings are kept for testing and for the last stage of training only.

Rigid teacherNewton 1.7, the Kamino hard-contact solver, 240 steps a second
Cloth teacherNewton's explicit integrator, 1,000–2,150 steps per frame
Rigid data50,048 episodes of 6 s, plus 12,800 on slopes (3.9 GB)
Cloth data20,032 episodes of 4 s, plus 1,024 held out (27 GB)
The recipe

Scene, material, action, simulate, check

Both generators follow the same five steps. Before recording, the teacher runs unrecorded (0.5 s for rigid scenes, 1.5 s for cloth) so that dropped objects and cloth have landed. Each gripper action is planned only when the previous one has finished, from where things actually are by then. Plans made in advance had the gripper come down on objects that had already moved.

Dataset 1 · rigid and hinged

Scanned household objects pushed, struck and dropped on a table

Each scene is a table with one to six scanned objects. Some are stacked, and most are dropped from 5–25 cm. Four scenes in ten also get a small cabinet with a drawer or a lid. A gripper then carries out up to four actions. It always goes for the object that has been touched least, so most objects get handled.

Objects
Scans from Google Scanned Objects: 780 tabletop-sized ones, 702 for training and 78 kept for testing. For collisions each is split into up to 12 convex parts. The network sees 48–600 points on each surface.
Materials
Friction 0.1–1.2, bounciness 0.05–0.85. Density 50–3,000 kg/m³ for solid objects; hollow shells (four in ten) get 1–5 mm walls. Six objects in ten hide one to three dense lumps, which move the centre of mass. Mass is kept between 5 g and 5 kg. Drawers and lids get a random damping.
Gripper
A 0.5 kg body that a controller pulls along the planned path, with its force capped at 60 N (5–15 N in a fifth of scenes). It is not moved directly: a gripper moved without any force limit crushed objects into the table with forces up to 303 kN.
Actions
Push, off-centre push, tilt and release, strike, knock one object into another, open a drawer or lid, sweep, random moves. In three episodes in ten an object also gets a kick that nothing visible causes: its velocity jumps by 0.1–1 m/s.
Recorded
6 s at 30 frames a second (181 frames): every body's position, orientation and velocity, the gripper's included; the force, point and torque between each pair of bodies in contact; the gripper's force, and whether it is at its cap. Surface points are rebuilt from the poses when training.
Slopes
A further 12,800 episodes, half of them with a 50 × 30 cm plank at 5–30° and one or two objects resting on it. They start at rest, so no object is ever released on a slope (see the limits below).
Cost
About 5,800 episodes per GPU-hour, 128 per file.
Why this teacher. Four of Newton's solvers were compared on the same checks. Kamino was the only one right on all of them: the law for a block sliding down a slope, no creep at rest, exact stacking and joint limits, bounce that does not depend on friction, and 0.69 mm from 93 real sliding objects. Isaac Lab's default, MuJoCo-Warp, was rejected because its soft contacts tie bounce to friction: a bouncy cube slid down a 20° slope it should rest on.
Dataset 2 · cloth

One towel, seven things a gripper does to it

Each scene is one towel or napkin on a table, sometimes over a scanned object or a tilted plank. A gripper grasps it and does one or two of seven things: lift and drop, lift and place, drag, fold, shake, push and slide. The overlay in the video gives each towel's size and material.

Cloth
15–50 cm per side (at most 2:1), a grid of points 1 cm apart: 256–2,601 points. It starts flat, over the edge, crumpled, draped on an object, held, or on a plank at 10–40°.
Materials
Weight 50–500 g/m², stretch stiffness 300–30,000 N/m, bending length 1.5–5 cm, friction 0.1–1.2, each drawn independently so that none can be guessed from another (except that the lightest cloths are never the stiffest).
Gripper
Moved directly along its path. A grasp holds up to 24 cloth points within 1.2 cm of the fingertips. The planner keeps the gripper above everything in the scene, and when placing it stops as soon as the cloth pushes back. A gripper driven into the plank pinched the cloth and made it explode.
Recorded
4 s at 30 frames a second (121 frames): every cloth point's position at full precision, the gripper's pose and planned path, the grasp force, and the force on the cloth from the table, the gripper and objects. Objects in cloth scenes never move.
Held out
1,024 test episodes, with obstacles never seen in training.
Cost
About 3,000 episodes per GPU-hour, 64 per file; the whole set took 6.8 h on one GPU.
Why this teacher. Newton's cloth solvers were run on one towel against formulas for how a strip hangs, how far a ledge droops, and how a towel slides. VBD and Style3D run stably, yet simulate a different cloth from the one they are given: VBD does not converge on light, stiff cloth, and Style3D resists turning. XPBD explodes. The explicit integrator passed all twelve checks. We added bending at the right strength, damping of stretching, contact with friction, the cloth's contact with itself (2 mm), and air drag.
Checks

Nothing is generated before the teacher passes, and nothing is used before the data does

A dataset inherits every defect of its teacher, so three kinds of check run first.

The teacher against known answers

Before any dataset is generated: the slide law within 2%, bounce within 0.05 of its label, momentum in free flight, drift at rest below 1 mm/s, penetration below 2 mm, and real sliding objects (Physics 101) within 2 mm. Cloth gets the same treatment from formulas.

Can the material be seen at all?

A network can only learn a parameter from motion if changing it changes the motion by more than the teacher's own noise. For cloth, weight changes what is seen in 92% of actions, bending 89%, stretch stiffness 80% and friction 68%. For rigid objects, measured on 128 episodes of the dataset: friction 61%, bounce 65%, mass 66%, centre of mass 68%, drawer and lid damping 82%.

The generator itself, on what it produces

The first 16,000 cloth episodes showed four defects the solver comparison had never tested. Towels bounced 3–5 cm off the table. 12% ended passing through themselves. Towels pinched by the gripper exploded, and light towels pressed into piles exploded too. All four were fixed in the generator's own teacher and checked there, and the dataset was regenerated. In the current set no episode explodes.

Limits

What the data does not contain, and what that costs

  • No camera, no video. Only 3D positions and forces. Video matters only upstream, to turn real recordings into mesh motion.
  • No object released on a slope. The slope episodes are recorded after objects have settled, so the data only shows objects staying put on slopes. A network without the physics layer therefore never slides on the real ramp (82 mm off, where the friction law alone is 1 mm). Most cloth starts on a plank also slide off during the unrecorded settling.
  • The teacher is not exactly repeatable. GPU arithmetic differs slightly from run to run. Once a towel is let go, those tiny differences grow: five restarts of one episode from the same state end 21–687 mm apart. Rollout errors below that spread mean the network memorised a recording, not that it is accurate.
  • Not yet built: ropes and soft objects (both teachers pass their checks), scenes mixing cloth with moving rigid objects, and objects held on a slope and let go.

Real data, for testing and the final stage

93 tracked slides of real objects down a ramp (Physics 101); eight real cloth recordings from PhysTwin, scored on hand-marked points; and 407 windows of a robot moving cloth from PGND, scored against the observed point clouds two and four seconds on.
