What each test of the learned simulator showed

The numbers, charts and videos behind the project briefing. Rigid objects first, then cloth, then what is running now. Every run had its prediction written down before it started, and the predictions that failed are reported as such.

How to read these.
  • One frame ahead is the network's error after one 1/30 s step from the true past, divided by the error of a simple guess: that everything keeps the speed it had. 1.00 is no better than the guess; lower is better.
  • Running alone (a replay or rollout): the network is shown the first eight frames, then predicts every later frame from its own output while the gripper follows its recorded path. Distances are in millimetres from the teacher simulator.
  • Unseen episodes were never trained on. Where two networks are compared, they are scored on the same episodes, and a difference is given with its 95% interval in brackets.

A plain transformer fits what the physics-layer network could not

The network that works is the simplest one we tried: the cloth network's attention blocks, 1.6M parameters, no physics layer, every input given. Trained for two and a half hours on 128 episodes, it predicts 32 unseen episodes one frame ahead at 0.57 of the simple guess's error (the guess that each object keeps its speed). That is level with two earlier baselines trained on all 50,000 episodes. Trained three times longer it reaches 0.50 and is ahead of them.

Training length sets the one-frame fit, and the amount of data sets how well the network runs alone. Eight times the episodes changed one frame at neither training length (0.57 against 0.54, then 0.45 against 0.45), and a network five times larger was worse at both training lengths (0.62 against 0.57, then 0.54 against 0.45): it learns more slowly per step, on its own episodes too. But the 128-episode network, trained longer, began to do better on its own episodes (0.41) than on unseen ones. The network trained on eight times the episodes does not (0.48 on its own, 0.45 on unseen), and its replays are closer: across the objects that move, 2.9 mm against 5.8 mm at two seconds at the median, closer in three quarters of them. Against the graph baseline trained on all 50,000 episodes it is ahead on both counts: by 0.13 [0.05, 0.23] on one frame and by 6.1 [3.8, 8.2] mm at two seconds. It is the first rigid network of ours clearly ahead of that baseline.

Nine times the training (108,000 steps, 22 hours on one GPU) takes the 1,024-episode network to 0.37 on the second unseen set and 0.38 on the first, lower than at three times by 0.10 [0.07, 0.14], better in 31 of 32 episodes, as we predicted (0.37 to 0.42). Its own episodes score 0.38, so there is still no gap and the data is not yet the limit. It is now ahead of the graph baseline by 0.24 [0.15, 0.34]. Running alone it is not closer. At one and two seconds it is level on average with the three-times network (the median object that moved is 4.0 mm off at two seconds against 2.9; we had predicted 2.0 to 2.9), and by the end it is 8 mm [4, 14] farther off on average, with the three-times network closer for three objects in four. The one-frame fit keeps improving with training; how well the network runs alone has stopped following it.

Running alone on unseen scenes, the 1,024-episode network ranges from near-exact to wrong. The clips below show that range, picked by rule from the 32 unseen scenes of the second set, not by eye. In its best scene it stays within 3.3 mm of the teacher for all six seconds while the gripper pushes an object between two shoes (the baseline drifts to 26 mm). In its best scene with four or more objects moving it follows a push to 1.6 mm for two seconds and ends 12 mm off. In its median scene it gets the gross motion and ends 39 mm off, with a box left tilted. In its two busiest scenes it follows for two seconds and for one, then misses what decides the ending: a standing book that the gripper knocks over, and objects pushed off the table's edge. The 128-episode version (last clips) follows its busiest scenes for about one second and ends further off than the baseline there (88 and 63 mm against 39 and 46), although across all moving objects it is level with the baseline or somewhat closer.

Two things about its training matter. It learns only with the training noise: without it the network stays at the simple guess (0.98). And it needs its inputs given. Our full recipe hides the material and the gripper's force part of the time and shortens the history, which is how the model is meant to learn to infer them. With that recipe the same network scores 0.77 and lets resting objects drift. The random turn of each scene, which was the ceiling for cloth, costs nothing here (0.55). Trained three times longer, it works as extra data: the network does worse on its own 128 episodes (0.46 against 0.41) and better on unseen ones (0.47 against 0.50 on the first set, 0.44 against 0.45 on the second), so no gap is left between the two, and objects that moved end closer to the teacher when it runs alone. So from 2026-11-24 we train with every input given and the random turn on, and return to hidden inputs once the simulator itself works.

Why the physics layer loses. Scored one frame ahead on unseen episodes, the physics layer on its own, with the network switched off, is 1.7 times worse than the simple guess. Our network trained on all 50,000 episodes is no better (1.8): training has not moved the layer's answer. The layer is exact for an object in flight and level with the simple guess for an object alone on the table. It fails where the gripper or a second object is involved, which is over half of the moving cases. There it applies the gripper's recorded force as if nothing pushed back: a 31-gram object pushed against another one is thrown 150 mm in a single frame. Giving the network a force of its own at the gripper did not fix this (1.5), and keeping the layer only where it is exact reached 0.78 to 0.81, behind the plain transformer.

What the layer still does better is hold things at rest. On the real ramp nothing without it slides: the plain transformer is 82 mm off, like the earlier baselines, where the layer's built-in friction alone is within 1 mm. Training it on ramp episodes does not change that (81 mm), as we predicted, but our reason was wrong. We said the episodes start settled, so the data never shows an object let go on a slope. In fact the ramp episodes set their objects down at rest on the slope at the first frame, and two thirds of them then slide. What training never shows is the real test's start: the network is given eight frames of rest and then has to begin the slide, while training windows start one frame after the let-go. A run that adds such windows is going (below). With drawers, doors and lids (52 in 128 unseen episodes), the plain transformer follows the ones the gripper moves as well as the physics-layer network does (11 mm against 9 mm; the graph baseline does not move them at all). But it lets the ones that should stay shut drift open by 27 mm, where the layer's joint holds them to 0.1 mm.

Cloth · c1 teacher data, towels and napkins, seven kinds of handling

The best cloth network so far, and where it stops

One frame ahead, the best cloth network removes about half of the simple guess's error (0.52), where the information it is given allows three quarters. Two things got it there. On 2026-11-22 we found that the original network had to re-learn every rotation; giving each vertex its surroundings in its own directions (the local frame) took the full 20,000-episode run from 0.69 to 0.59, ahead in every kind of handling. Then training length: the same network trained for as long again reaches 0.52, lower by 0.07 [0.06, 0.08] in every kind of handling, with no gap between its own episodes (0.54) and unseen ones. More data does not help: 2,000 episodes at the full training budget score 0.60 against 0.59 for all 20,000. A third stretch of training (three times the full budget in all) takes it to 0.49, lower by 0.03 [0.03, 0.04] in every kind of handling, as we predicted (0.475 to 0.50), still with no gap to its own episodes (0.50). Running alone it is closer at two and 3.8 seconds (54 and 78 mm against 57 and 85) and keeps most cloths' shape better, but more cloths blow up: 21 of the 336 unseen episodes end above twice their area, against 6 before. The checkpoint 4,000 steps earlier says this is real, not one checkpoint's luck, as we predicted: 27 blow-ups there, against 12 for the second stretch's matching checkpoint (paired, the area grows by 0.6 [0.3, 1.0] times more on average, while the cloth is 4 mm [1, 7] closer at two seconds). The second stretch's own count swings between 6 and 12. The blow-ups are explosions, not slight stretch: in the worst unseen episodes the cloth ends at 9 to 18 times its area. How the cause of the fit was found is told below, after the real-cloth results.

Rolled out alone on unseen episodes, the longer-trained network is 15, 57 and 85 mm from the teacher after one, two and 3.8 seconds, against 18, 65 and 97 before the extra training and 24, 80 and 122 for the original network. It stretches the cloth by 6% on average (1% at the median), half of what it did before the extra training. It was the best cloth network we had on every measure until the third stretch below. The four videos of the full-data runs below show it beside the original network.

The extra training on teacher data also carries to real cloth. Before any real data, the longer-trained local-frame network is 25.6 and 34.7 mm from the observed cloth at 2 and 4 seconds, closer than before the extra training by 5.6 [2.2, 9.5] and 10.2 [2.0, 19.0] mm, with the same small stretch. A third stretch of training gives 24.4 and 32.4 mm, inside what we predicted (22 to 25 and 30 to 36) but no clearer a gain: 1.2 [−1.5, 3.7] and 2.3 [−3.3, 8.2] mm closer. The blow-ups show on real cloth too: by 4 seconds its cloth is stretched 30% and grown to 3.4 times its area, against 6% and 1.3 times.

That lead did not survive the same real fine-tuning. We predicted it would end level with the shorter-trained network's 17.6 mm; it ended at 26.2 and 28.9 mm at 2 and 4 seconds, 8.6 [5.9, 11.8] and 9.1 [6.0, 12.3] mm farther from the real cloth, with its cloth stretched 11% and grown to 1.6× its area (the shorter-trained network: 2% and 1.0×). The fine-tune itself went unstable. Within 200 steps the cloth in its real training windows was stretched to 150% where the earlier fine-tune kept it near 5%, and its gradients were 10 to 200 times larger, with the same settings (learning rate 1e-4, gradients through the last 10 frames). So the result says that this fine-tune's settings do not carry to the better network. It does not say that a better teacher fit is worth nothing on real cloth. Whether a gentler fine-tune keeps the lead is not tested.

Reading material from motion, the second claim, has not appeared. The cloth model predicts almost the same motion whether its material is given or hidden, and reads only stretchiness, partly, from motion. Even that reading comes from stretches of a hundredth of a percent, which exist only in simulation: take away the network's finest stretch measurement and it guesses no better than chance.

The current problem: the network fits its own teacher loosely

Newest finding (2026-11-22). A deliberately small test changed the picture. Trained on a single episode, the network could not fit even that episode: its error stayed at 0.64 of the simple guess's, the same as on all 20,000 episodes. Yet it memorises one moment of the episode almost exactly, and eight moments well. What breaks it is rotation. Training turns every example by a random angle, and the same eight moments, turned at random each time, stay stuck at 0.71. The network describes every motion in fixed world directions, so the same physical situation facing another way is a new example it has to learn again. With the turning switched off, the unchanged trainer fits the episode to 0.10.

The fix is to give each cloth vertex its surroundings, and take its force, in the vertex's own directions, so that a turned scene is the identical input. Through the unchanged trainer, turning included, this version fits the episode to 0.09 (chart below). A first version lost something on the way: with no fixed "up" it did not learn to hold cloth against gravity on episodes it had not seen (5 mm of vertical error per frame, where the original network left 1.5). Giving it the vertical back, which a turn about the vertical leaves alone, repaired that.

Fitting each frame is not the same as replaying the episode. A network that has only seen the teacher's exact states multiplies its own first error about four times per frame, and the cloth flies apart within half a second. Trained with small random errors added to its inputs, the networks replay the episode. To know how good a replay can be at all, we restarted the teacher simulator itself from the same recorded frame, five times, changing nothing (chart below). Like the networks, it is given the cloth's positions but not its exact speeds, so it begins about a millimetre off. While the gripper holds the towel, the five restarts stay 2 to 7 mm from the recording. Within a third of a second of the release they scatter, and they end 21 to 687 mm away: a falling towel amplifies differences in rounding, so the recorded ending is one of many equally valid ones.

Against that yardstick, after the short training of the chart above the local-frame network stays 3 to 18 mm from the recording while the towel is held, and its cloth stretches by 16 to 62%. Trained three times longer, with the random turning still on, it fits each frame to 0.04 of the simple guess's error and replays the episode within about a millimetre for the whole 2.4 seconds the towel is held, with 3 to 4% stretch. That completes the proof at the smallest scale: the network and its physics layer can fit a teacher episode and reproduce it on their own, for as long as the episode can be reproduced at all. After the release this network ends 13 mm from the recording, closer than any of the teacher's restarts. It has memorised this one film, as the original network did at one fixed orientation (19 mm), and it needed about 460 passes over the episode to do it. A memorised episode therefore says nothing about unseen cloth, and memorising cannot be how 20,000 episodes are learned. Rollouts of episodes a network has never seen, averaged over many, are the test that counts.

Two larger steps followed the same day. On sixteen episodes at equal training, the local-frame network with the vertical is the best of four versions on its own episodes: 0.40 of the simple guess's error and still falling, against 0.70 for the original, 0.53 for the original with the turning switched off and 0.59 for the local-frame version without the vertical. On 2,000 episodes, scored on episodes neither network saw, it is ahead of the original at every point of equal training (chart below): 0.95 against 1.16 after 3,000 steps, 0.87 against 0.97 after 6,000, 0.78 against 0.87 after 9,000 and 0.75 against 0.87 at the end. The original stopped improving after 9,000 steps and the local-frame network did not, so at equal training it ends lower, not only sooner. An earlier input change had led early and ended level; this one does not follow that pattern.

Rolled out alone on 336 unseen episodes at the end of training, the local-frame network is about a third closer to the teacher: 26 mm against 43 after one second, 79 against 135 after two, 115 against 171 at the end, and closer in about three episodes of four. That equals the rollouts of our best original network, which had ten times the episodes and eight times the training (24, 80 and 122 mm on the same episodes), although its one-frame fit is still behind that network's (0.75 against 0.67). Neither is a usable simulator over seconds yet, and the videos under the chart show how each fails: the original network's cloth typically shrinks into a crumple, the local-frame network's typically keeps its size and, in a minority of episodes, blows up instead.

On the strength of the 6,000-step results the full 20,000-episode run of the local-frame network started on 2026-11-22 and ended on 2026-11-23 (second chart below). It ends at 0.59 of the simple guess's error on unseen episodes, against 0.69 for the original at the same training and 0.67 for the original trained twice as long, and it is ahead in every kind of handling. Our written prediction was wrong in the good direction: we expected it to reach the original's level sooner (0.66), not to end below it. Its lead was small in the middle of the run (level at 8,000 steps), which it never was on 2,000 episodes; the data is not the reason, because the small training budget gives the same result on 2,000 and on all 20,000 episodes (0.75 both; third chart below). That leaves the training schedule: the full run takes twice the examples per step and lowers its learning rate over twice as many steps, and the local-frame network's gain seems to come with the steps and the falling learning rate. Rolled out alone on 336 unseen episodes, it is 18, 65 and 97 mm from the teacher after one, two and 3.8 seconds, against 24, 80 and 122 for the original trained twice as long, and closer in about seven episodes of ten. It stretches the cloth far less (12% on average against 39%; 3% against 24% in the typical episode) and keeps the cloth's area where the original's typically shrinks to two thirds. This is the first time a better one-frame fit has come with better rollouts: three earlier networks with one-frame fits of 0.78, 0.75 and 0.67 all rolled out to about 25, 79 and 115 to 122 mm. It is still not a fit, and 97 mm after 3.8 seconds is not a usable simulator.

Data size matters little at the small budget. With 200 episodes instead of 2,000 and the same training, the local-frame network scores 0.78 on unseen episodes instead of 0.75, and fits its own episodes equally well at both sizes (0.72 and 0.74). With all 20,000 episodes it scores 0.75 again, as we had predicted in writing. Even on 200 episodes it is ahead of the original on 2,000. At the full budget the answer is the same: 2,000 episodes score 0.60 and all 20,000 score 0.59 (chart below), so generating more cloth data is not the next step. On rigid objects dropping the physics layer was what made the network fit, so we tried the same on cloth: the local-frame network on 2,000 episodes with and without its layer, trained identically. Without the layer it starts ahead on one frame (by 0.04 after a quarter and half of the training) and ends level (0.75 against 0.75). It is behind by 0.02 to 0.03 in five kinds of handling and far ahead only when the gripper pushes the cloth along the table (0.77 against 0.92), the one kind where the layer has always done worst. Running alone it is much worse: 35, 110 and 191 mm after one, two and 3.8 seconds against 26, 79 and 115, with more cloths blowing up. For cloth the layer does not limit the fit and keeps the rollouts stable, so it stays.

Every input given: as close, and the cloth keeps its shape. Our usual recipe hides the material half the time and the gripper's force some of the time, and gives the network a random number of past frames. Trained instead with all of them always given, the same network fits one frame as well (0.76 against 0.75, as we predicted). Running alone it is as close or a little closer: 24, 77 and 107 mm off after one, two and 3.8 seconds against 26, 79 and 115 (closer by 1.8 [0.3, 3.3] mm at one second; the later differences are within noise). And its cloth keeps its shape: it stretches 12% on average against 61%, and ends at 1.06 times its area against 2.2, less stretched in four of five unseen episodes and in every kind of handling. We had predicted level. With everything else given, a random number of past frames brings the stretch back (98%, area 3.7 times), so the always-full history is what helps. From 2026-11-25 cloth trains with every input given, as rigid objects do. A run now tests this on the best network (see Running now).

A first score of these networks said the opposite (64 mm at one second, cloth at five times its area). It was a scoring mistake: the scorer hid the material during rollouts from networks that had never been trained without it. Each network is now scored on the inputs it trained with.

WorldParticle as a baseline: not obtained. WorldParticle (2026, 157M parameters) was trained with its published settings on the same 2,000 episodes for the same 7.2 GPU-hours as our network. It learned nothing: on every window its next frame is the constant-velocity guess (1.00, against our 0.75). Its learned correction is one upward nudge, the same on every point, that cancels its own gravity step and nothing else. It cannot fit even one episode: five one-episode runs (a one-step or five-step loss, 3,000 or 10,000 steps, half or full precision, two learning rates) all end at 1.00 with the same constant. Its input layer does see the cloth (17 neighbours a point on average). The release has no training code, so the training loop is ours, and the failure may be in how we train or feed it rather than in the network. This says nothing about WorldParticle yet.

The four episodes were fixed before we looked at any local-frame rollout of them. Across all 336 unseen episodes the extra training brings the rollouts 12 mm closer at 3.8 seconds; on these four it helps the fold and the push, and costs on the shake and the drop.

The third stretch, watched. On the same four fixed episodes the third stretch of training is level with the second on the fold, the drop and the shake, and fixes the push: the towel slides off the edge as the teacher's does and keeps its shape as it falls (130 mm off at the end, area 0.95 times), where the second stretch's grew to four times its area. Its blow-ups are elsewhere. On an unseen lift-and-place where it scored 17 times the area, both networks drop the towel short of where the teacher lays it and crumple it; the crumpled part of the third stretch's towel then grows into a spiky ball (9 times its area by 4 seconds, the second stretch's 2.2). The explosion starts where the cloth lies folded on itself, after it lands, not from the start. Whether a rollout explodes also turns on small changes: these videos give the network the material, which the score hid, and an unseen fold that scored 15 times its area does not blow up at all in its video (0.9 times). The runs sit near an instability, and the third stretch is nearer.

The yardstick is a guess with no physics at all: each point keeps the velocity it had (constant velocity). The teacher simulator, restarted from exactly what the network is shown, three frames of positions, removes three quarters of that guess's error. The network removes about a third. Since 2026-11-06 we have been ruling causes out one by one: data size, the noise added to its inputs in training, the loss, the network type, the frame rate, contact, long-range coupling and numeric precision were each tested and did not change the fit. Neither does size: a network eight times larger, trained on as many examples, fits the same. Training twice as long helps a little (0.67 to 0.65). Its error on training episodes equals its error on held-out ones, so more of the same data would not help. Nor is it the material: the network ignores the material it is given, yet its error is the same whether every cloth has one material or materials vary widely. A teacher that knew only the range of materials would do worse than the network on mixed materials and better on a single one. Trained on one material only, it fits that material no better than a network trained on all of them. Nor does the next frame need impossibly precise inputs: the teacher, restarted from positions each shaken randomly by 0.1 mm, still removes 70% of the guess's error. The network's error is not random noise. It gets the pattern of each frame's motion right but only about two thirds of its size. The shortfall is the same everywhere: across the cloth and along it, at its edges and in its middle, for every kind of motion and every material. Cloth falling after a release fits best and slow cloth dragged by the gripper worst, but every group falls between 0.53 and 0.77. Nudge its inputs and it responds in the right direction, but only about 60% as strongly as the teacher does. It also reads stretch to a tenth of a micrometre, a precision that exists only in simulation. Trained on inputs shaken by 10 micrometres it fits almost as well (0.01 worse) and holds up just as well over seconds and on real cloth, so that precision is not what sets the fit. We do not yet know why it acts at 60% strength.

One input change helps clearly: giving the network each vertex's surrounding patch of the mesh, in that vertex's own frame of reference. With the same short training, cloth rolled out for 3.8 s ends 124 mm from the teacher instead of 209 mm, and stretches 6.6% instead of 35%. With a sixth of the training, it is level over seconds with the fully trained network (125 mm), and its cloth keeps its shape far better (6.6% stretch against 28%). With full training its one-frame advantage is gone, but over seconds it still wins: after 3.8 s the cloth is 109 mm from the teacher against 123 mm, and it stretches 5.5% against 29% (the teacher: 0.01%).

Already ruled out for the cloth fit

At the full training budget, twice the training (worth 0.02), a larger network, the patch input, the material and the training noise have each been ruled out as what holds the fit (removing the noise gains 0.01). A test on cloth that always has the same material went wrong: with every material equal, a side output that guesses the material grew overconfident, and its learning signal, 50-100 times the usual size, drowned out the main one, so the network got worse even on its own training data. An earlier one-material test had the same fault, so its conclusion is withdrawn. Rerun with that side output cut off from the rest of the network, one material fits no better than many (so the material is not it), and the cut itself is a small gain we keep. Full numeric precision changed nothing, and the missing motion is not one mechanism such as waves. Nor is the frame rate: a network that steps at 120 frames per second instead of 30, trained three times longer, still predicts one 1/30 s frame better (0.96 against 1.32 mm) and keeps the cloth less stretched, but four frames ahead the two are level, so recording data at a higher frame rate is not the lever. Nor is the simulation-only micrometre precision of its stretch reading: denied it, the network fits within 0.01. Removing the stretch reading altogether, though, makes cloth over-stretch over seconds, so it stays. What is left is slow progress with more training, about 0.02 per doubling.

Both tracks · running alone · 2026-11-25

Practising longer on its own output does not help

Both networks learn to run alone only through a short drill in training: for the first few frames of each training window they are fed their own predictions instead of the teacher's, up to 4 frames for cloth and 6 for rigid objects. We made that drill longer, in short fine-tunes of the current best networks, each beside a control fine-tune of the same length that keeps the drill as it was.

Cloth. The more frames practised alone, the more rollouts blow up: 25, 31 and 44 of 336 unseen episodes at 4, 8 and 16 frames. We predicted fewer. The one-frame fit stays level (+0.006), and positions after 2 s are slightly closer (3 mm), but the mean stretch doubles (42% against 22%).

Rigid objects. Practising 16 frames costs 0.02–0.03 on one frame and leaves replays where they were. Practising 30 frames (one second) costs 0.05 and brings the objects that moved a few millimetres closer by the end (4 and 10 mm on the two unseen sets, both intervals through zero). That is the same exchange as training a third as long, not a new lever.

Watched. In both videos below the cloth first bunches up as it lands or folds, and the bunched part then grows into a spiky ball within about a second, as in the third stretch's own blow-ups. One reading: in this drill the target is still the teacher's next frame, which a cloth that has drifted can reach only by pulling unevenly, so the network learns corrections that stretch. The loss on the network's own 2–3 s runs, now running, has the same target and may show the same; a loss on the cloth's shape alone would not.

The two episodes were picked from those where the 16-frame network blew up and the control did not. A third such episode, a shake, does not blow up in its video (1.6 times its area against 13.6 when scored): these rollouts sit near an instability, and small differences decide whether they cross it.

A loss on the network's own runs. The next trial adds a loss on the network's own 2 s and 3.3 s runs, measured against the teacher's, in 400-step fine-tunes beside a control without it. The 2 s arm is done. It runs closer: 2.6 mm [1.1, 4.2] at 2 s and 3.5 mm [−0.3, 7.2] at 3.8 s, for a one-frame cost of 0.004. But it blows up as often as the control (32 against 30 of 336) and stretches as much. We predicted a third fewer blow-ups. The 3.3 s arm is due at about 23:00 UTC.

Both trials fine-tuned the third stretch that was trained with the material hidden, giving it every input for 400 to 3,000 steps. Their controls kept 25 to 30 blow-ups, so a short fine-tune does not undo the tail. The third stretch trained with every input given throughout has 5 (see the cloth section).
